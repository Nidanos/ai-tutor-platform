1. Purpose and Method
This survey looks at two sets of work and compares them.
The first is research on how people learn. Over roughly forty years, repeated experiments have
shown the conditions under which learning lasts. These results are well accepted in the field.
The second is the set of products and research systems used to teach technical subjects. For each one
we checked which of the research findings it actually uses.
When we put the two side by side, we found that the methods known to produce lasting learning are
mostly missing from the products sold to teach. The reason seems to be commercial, not technical.
This gap is the main justification for our project.
We chose sources on three grounds: well-cited foundational papers, recent experiments on
generative AI in learning, and commercial products available in 2026.

2. Foundational Research on Learning
2.1 Bloom's two-sigma problem
Bloom, B. S. (1984). The 2 Sigma Problem: The Search for Methods of Group Instruction as
Effective as One-to-One Tutoring. Educational Researcher, 13(6), 4–16.
Bloom found that students who got one-to-one tutoring together with mastery learning scored about
two standard deviations above students taught in a normal classroom. That means the average
tutored student did better than about 98% of the classroom group. Bloom presented this as a
problem: one-to-one tutoring works, but it is too expensive to give everyone, so the challenge is to
find methods that get close to it for large groups.
How it relates to our project. Bloom's result sets the target. Every system in this survey, ours
included, tries to get close to one-to-one tutoring at scale. We take two ideas directly from Bloom's
study: mastery learning, where a student moves on only after showing mastery of the current topic,
and individual pacing. Our learner model is a way of making the mastery check automatic.

2.2 The testing effect
Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). The Power of Testing Memory: Basic Research and
Implications for Educational Practice. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 1(3), 181–210.
Roediger and Karpicke showed that trying to recall information builds stronger memory than
reading it again. Students who read a passage once and then practised recalling it remembered 61%
after a week. Students who read it four times remembered 40%. The re-reading group also expected
to do better, so their confidence was the opposite of their actual result.
How it relates to our project. This is why our system updates mastery only from evidence of
producing something, never from reading or watching. It also explains the problem we started with:
watching an instructor is re-exposure, and re-exposure builds confidence without building skill. The
exercise ladder in synopsis §3.5 is a structured form of recall practice.

2.3 Desirable difficulties
Bjork, R. A., & Bjork, E. L. (2020). Desirable Difficulties in Theory and Practice. Journal of
Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, 9(4), 475–479. Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2011).
Making Things Hard on Yourself, But in a Good Way: Creating Desirable Difficulties to Enhance
Learning. In Psychology and the Real World, 56–64.
The Bjorks separate two things: how well a student performs while practising, and how well they
remember and apply it later. These often move in opposite directions. Spacing, mixing topics,
changing practice conditions and testing all slow down practice and cause more mistakes, but they
lead to better long-term learning. Practice that feels smooth tends to do the reverse.
How it relates to our project. Our main design rule comes from this. If some difficulty during
practice helps learning, then a tutor that removes all difficulty is working against its own goal. The
hint ladder, the refusal to give solution code and the planned reduction of support are all ways of
keeping a useful level of difficulty. This work also explains why products avoid these methods: they
feel worse in the moment, so products judged on user satisfaction are pushed away from them.

2.4 The spacing effect
Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed Practice in
Verbal Recall Tasks: A Review and Quantitative Synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354–380.
This meta-analysis covers 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 184 articles. It shows that
spreading practice over time gives better retention than doing it all at once. More useful for us, it
shows that the best gap between study sessions grows with how long the material needs to be
remembered. So spacing is not one fixed schedule; it depends on the goal.
How it relates to our project. Our decay model is based on this. The system stores a decay rate for
each skill and reduces the mastery score exponentially with the time since the last evidence. When
the score drops below a threshold, the skill goes back into the revision queue. Cepeda et al. support
both having a decay term and letting its rate differ by type of material.

2.5 Worked examples and the expertise reversal effect
Sweller, J., & Cooper, G. A. (1985). The Use of Worked Examples as a Substitute for Problem
Solving in Learning Algebra. Cognition and Instruction, 2(1), 59–89. Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive
Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning. Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257–285.
Sweller and Cooper found that beginners who studied worked examples did better than beginners
who solved the same problems alone. Solving without guidance uses up working memory on
searching for a method, which leaves less capacity for actually learning the pattern. Later studies
found the expertise reversal effect: as students get better, worked examples help less and less, and
eventually solving problems alone becomes the better option.
How it relates to our project. Put together, these results say that support should change over time.
Support that suits a beginner can hold back an intermediate student. This is why we have a five-level
ladder that starts with examples and prediction tasks and ends with building from scratch, and why
support is reduced on a schedule. Keeping support at one fixed level would be wrong for most of a
student's progress.

2.6 Knowledge tracing
Corbett, A. T., & Anderson, J. R. (1994). Knowledge Tracing: Modeling the Acquisition of
Procedural Knowledge. User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction, 4(4), 253–278.
Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) treats each skill as a hidden yes/no variable (mastered or not)
and updates it from the student's answers using a hidden Markov model. It has four parameters: prior
knowledge, learning rate, slip probability and guess probability. It is used in the Cognitive Tutor
systems and is still the standard method in this area.
How it relates to our project, and where we differ. We follow the general idea of BKT: estimate
hidden skill from noisy evidence, taking context into account. We differ from classic BKT in three
ways:
1. Two scores per skill. BKT has one mastery variable per skill. We track reading and writing
ability separately because they can be very different. A profile of 10/11 on reading and 0/4 on
writing averages to about 0.65, which does not tell you what the student should do next.
2. Forgetting over time. Classic BKT has no forgetting, so once a skill is mastered it stays
mastered in the model. We add exponential decay, as described in §2.4.
3. Only execution counts as evidence. We use only code that was run against tests, correct
predictions of program behaviour, and solved exercises. Self-reports and content completion are
not used at all.

2.7 How well tutoring systems work
VanLehn, K. (2011). The Relative Effectiveness of Human Tutoring, Intelligent Tutoring Systems,
and Other Tutoring Systems. Educational Psychologist, 46(4), 197–221.
VanLehn reviewed controlled comparisons and reported two results. The effect of human tutoring (d
= 0.79) is much smaller than Bloom's two sigma. Intelligent tutoring systems (d = 0.76) come very

3

close to human tutors. He found that the main difference between systems is the level at which they
interact: systems that respond to each step or part of a step do better than those that only check the
final answer.
How it relates to our project. This shapes our design. A system that only marks the final
submission right or wrong loses most of the benefit. Our hint ladder responds to the student's current
attempt, we detect when a student is stuck during an exercise instead of after it, and the difficulty
level can change mid-task. VanLehn's numbers also show that a well-made automated tutor can
come close to a human one, which makes our goal realistic.

3. Recent Studies on Generative AI in Learning
3.1 Unrestricted AI help harms learning
Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI
Without Guardrails Can Harm Learning: Evidence from High School Mathematics. Proceedings of
the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS).
This is the study closest to our project, so we describe it in some detail.
It was a randomised controlled trial with nearly 1,000 students in grades 9 to 11 at a large high
school in Turkey, during the Fall 2023–24 semester. There were three groups:
Group

What they got

GPT Base

Unrestricted access to a GPT-4 chat interface while practising

GPT Tutor

GPT-4 limited to teacher-designed hints instead of answers

Control

No AI help

What they found:
• While practising with AI available, both AI groups did better than the control group.
• When AI was taken away for the exam, the students who had used unrestricted GPT-4 scored
17% lower than students who never had AI at all.
• Students used the unrestricted model as a crutch, getting answers instead of working them out.
• The GPT Tutor group, limited to hints, largely avoided this harm. They kept the benefit during
practice without becoming dependent.
How it relates to our project. This study supports our main idea with a controlled experiment. It
shows that unrestricted AI help damages learning while making students look and feel more
successful during practice, which is the problem described in synopsis §2.2. It also shows that hintbased help, which is what we propose, reduces that harm.
We go further than the study in three ways. Our hints are spread over five levels instead of one hint
policy. The limit is enforced by the server (stored hint level and output filter), not only by the
prompt, since prompt-only limits can be worked around. And help is reduced over time instead of
staying the same.

3.2 Completion rates in online courses
Reich, J., & Ruipérez-Valiente, J. A. (2019). The MOOC Pivot. Science, 363(6423), 130–131.
The authors studied 565 MOOCs from MIT and Harvard on edX over 6.5 years, with 12.67 million
registrations. Completion among all registered students was 3.13% in 2017–18, down from about
4% in the two years before and almost 6% in 2014–15. 52% of registered students never started the
course. Completion did not improve over six years, even among students who said they intended to
finish or who paid for a verified certificate.
How it relates to our project. Online technical courses struggle even on the simple measure of
completion. More important for us, none of these providers report what students who finished can
actually do. Completion is the final number the industry reports, and it only measures consumption.
Our system measures what students can produce and ignores completion entirely.

The Bastani et al. trial (§3.1) tested this kind of tool directly and found a 17% drop in performance

Khanmigo shows that a tutor which does not give answers can work commercially at large scale,
which lowers the risk of our central idea. There are still three differences. As far as we can tell its
limit is set in the prompt, which a determined user can get around; ours is enforced on the server and
by an output filter. Its support does not reduce over time, which §2.5 says is wrong for a student who
is improving. And it sits on top of a linear course instead of a prerequisite graph, so it cannot skip
known topics or choose the next step from the student's level.
We included Duolingo Max because it shows the incentive problem most clearly. Streaks and XP are
there to bring users back every day, and its known limits (short scripted conversations, no
pronunciation scoring) suggest a product built for daily return more than for final ability.

6. Research Gap
We draw three conclusions from the survey.
Each method we use already has research behind it. Recall practice, spacing, desirable difficulty,
fading support, knowledge tracing and step-level interaction are not new. Each has decades of

8

experimental support. Our contribution is to combine all of them in one system, which the table
above shows has not been done.
Two of our design choices are new, and both are based on evidence. Keeping reading and writing
mastery separate does not appear in the knowledge tracing literature, which uses one score per skill.
We do it because the two scores can differ so much that an average gives a misleading next step.
Reducing support on a planned schedule, as part of the student's plan and not only on request,
follows from the expertise reversal effect, but none of the systems we surveyed does it.
The gap is mainly commercial. None of the methods in Section 2 is hard to build. Spacing is one
exponential term, and a hint ladder is a number stored on the server. Products leave them out
because each one lowers engagement: the product feels harder, and users finish and leave sooner. A
product judged on time in the app, daily active users or satisfaction ratings would hurt its own
numbers by adopting them.
This is why a new product can take this approach where existing ones cannot. We treat time spent in
the app as something to reduce, as long as students gain the same ability. The Bastani et al. trial
suggests that ignoring this costs students about 17% of what they would otherwise retain.
